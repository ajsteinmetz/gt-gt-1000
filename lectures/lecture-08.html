Someone is going to look you up

Who searches for you?

  • Recruiters, after you hand them a resume at the career fair
  • Research group leaders, before they reply to your email
  • REU, scholarship, and study abroad committees
  • Your Team Project teammates

Try it now (1 minute)

  1. Open a private browser window.
  2. Search your full name, and your name plus “Georgia Tech.”
  3. What comes up first? Is it what you want a recruiter to see?

You can’t stop people from searching. You can decide what they find.

Take a couple of reactions. Common results: nothing at all, a high school sports page, someone else with the same name. All of these are fixable, and a complete LinkedIn profile usually lands near the top of a name search. Also worth a minute this week: check the privacy settings on personal accounts (Instagram, TikTok, etc.). Private is fine; it doesn’t need to be professional, it just shouldn’t be the first thing a recruiter finds.

Building your LinkedIn profile

  • Photo: clear, smiling, plain background
  • Headline: who you are, what interests you
  • Summary: a short paragraph
  • Education: Georgia Tech and your major
  • Experience and skills you actually have
  • Custom URL: linkedin.com/in/yourname

Less effective headline

Student at Georgia Institute of Technology

More effective headline

Physics first-year at Georgia Tech · Interested in quantum materials · Python, Arduino

Source: (Georgia Institute of Technology, n.d.)

The headline appears under your name everywhere: search results, comments, connection requests. “Student” tells the reader nothing. If you’re looking for something (a summer internship, an undergraduate research position), say so in the headline. For the photo, a phone camera, a plain wall, and good daylight are enough. The custom URL is under “Edit public profile & URL”; use it on your resume too.

What counts when you’re a first-year

  • High school projects you are proud of
  • Jobs, including ones unrelated to physics
  • Volunteering, clubs, and teams
  • This semester’s Team Project
  • Skills: Python, LaTeX, lab equipment, CAD
  • You already wrote most of this: start from your resume.
  • Add a sentence or two of context to each entry.
  • Attach media: a poster, a photo of a build, a link to code.

Show, don’t claim. Skip “motivated,” “driven,” and “problem solver”; let what you did say it.

Nobody expects a first-year to have a long work history. A summer job at a restaurant shows reliability and working with people. Projects and media are evidence you can do the work. The summary can be pieced together from resume bullets rather than written as a long bio.

Beyond LinkedIn

Platform Good for When
GitHub Code, data analysis, class and personal projects Now, if you write code
Personal website A portfolio of projects; GitHub Pages and Quarto are free Any time
ORCID A permanent ID that links you to your research When you join a research group
Google Scholar Your publications and citations When you publish

All optional for the assignment, and none are worth extra points. Add them when they help.

This course website is built with Quarto and hosted on GitHub Pages, so that is a working example of a free personal site. ORCID is often requested when you submit an abstract or paper; there is no rush to make one now.

Connecting without being weird

  • Personalize every invitation: where you met and why you’re connecting.
  • Connect within a day of meeting someone: a recruiter, a speaker, an alum.
  • Join groups such as Georgia Tech Alumni.
  • Put your profile URL on your resume and in your email signature.

“Hi Dr. Lee, I’m a first-year Physics major in GT 1000. I enjoyed your Physics@Tech talk today, especially the part on detector calibration. I’d like to keep up with your group’s work.”

Source: (Georgia Institute of Technology, n.d.)

Back to the comic: an invitation with no note reads like spam, and some people decline those. A two-sentence note is enough. Today’s Physics@Tech speaker is a good first connection. Update the example to match the actual speaker and topic, or keep it generic (“Dr. Lee” is made up).
